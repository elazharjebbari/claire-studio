\begin{abstract}
The Annex of Directive 93/13/EEC lists seventeen kinds of contractual terms that may be regarded as unfair: the grey list. Automated detectors of unfair terms do not use it. They classify sentences with learned models whose decisions cite no legal ground. We ask what happens when the grey list is executed literally. Each clause of a contract, located by a human-validated theme, is described by a clause template stating who may do what, under which conditions, with what notice and what remedy; templates are extracted by a language model that sees neither the queries nor the reference labels. Grey-list items become queries over these templates, frozen with a recorded hash before any computation on held-out contracts, so that the result measures the list rather than our ability to fit it. Eleven of the seventeen items are expressible as structural conditions. On 17 held-out \corpus{} contracts, the frozen queries are precise on several items (0.61 to 0.83) but retrieve a third of the unfair sentences: their binary $F_1$ of 0.36 is below that of the clause theme alone (0.44) and of a fine-tuned legal encoder (0.70). Every flag cites the item, the template fields and the sentences that triggered it, and the error analysis locates where literal execution and the benchmark diverge. Queries, templates and code are released.
\end{abstract}
