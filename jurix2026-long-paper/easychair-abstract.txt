The Annex of Directive 93/13/EEC, known as the grey list, describes the kinds of contractual terms that may be regarded as unfair. Automated detectors of unfair terms in online terms of service do not use it: they classify sentences with learned models whose decisions cite no legal ground. We ask what happens when the grey list is executed literally. Each clause of a contract is described by a simple template recording who may do what, under which conditions, with what notice and with what remedy for the other party. Templates are extracted by a language model that sees neither the queries nor the reference labels. Grey-list items are then written as queries over these templates and frozen before any evaluation, so that the result measures the list itself rather than our ability to fit it. On held-out contracts from the CLAUDETTE corpus, most items can be expressed this way, and the queries are precise on several of them. They retrieve, however, only a minority of the annotated unfair terms, and their overall performance stays below that of the clause theme alone and well below that of a fine-tuned legal language model. In exchange, every flag cites the grey-list item, the template fields and the sentences that triggered it, and the error analysis shows where a literal reading of the Annex and the benchmark diverge. Queries, templates and code are released.
