Two consequences follow for annotation practice. First, the reliability cost of
multi-label annotation need not be paid by abandoning multi-label annotation: it
can be recovered by consolidating the taxonomy, which preserves the ability to
record that a clause does two things at once. Second, that consolidation cannot be
delegated to the agreement signal. Merging along measured confusion clusters is
what a data-driven procedure would do, and on this corpus it selects precisely the
taxonomy that collapses the legal distinction the downstream task depends on. The
criterion has to come from outside the annotation statistics --- here, from the
strata of unfairness that consumer protection law itself draws
\cite{eu1993directive,micklitz2017empire}. For the evaluation of language models,
the gap between the human ceiling and the best judge (0.859 against 0.581) is
large enough that judges are not substitutable for trained annotators on this
task, in line with findings on other legal annotation tasks
\cite{thalken2023legal,bavaresco2025judges}; a compact domain-specific encoder
\cite{chalkidis2020legalbert,dominguezolmedo2025lawma} closes much, though not
all, of that gap.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Pre-annotations were post-edited rather than produced from scratch, and annotator
divergence from the suggested label (38.5--48.6\% of sentences) bounds the
anchoring risk \cite{choi2024llmeffect} without eliminating it. The layer covers
one legal system and one language. The judges are scored on stored
pre-annotations, so the benchmark measures a historical output rather than the
current state of the art. The fine-tuned encoder is a single model with early
stopping on the test fold, making its reported agreement mildly optimistic.
Finally, the aggregated gold carries only 1.04 labels per sentence, so a
multi-label score computed on it would not measure the difficulty of the task as
annotated; the multi-label cost reported here is measured on individual votes,
where the secondary themes survive.
