Two consequences follow for annotation practice. First, the reliability cost of
multi-label annotation need not be paid by abandoning multi-label annotation: it
can be recovered by consolidating the taxonomy, which preserves the ability to
record that a clause does two things at once. Second, that consolidation cannot be
delegated to the agreement signal. On this corpus, a purely data-driven procedure
selects precisely the taxonomy that collapses the legal distinction the downstream
task depends on, so the criterion has to come from outside the annotation
statistics \cite{eu1993directive,micklitz2017empire}. For the evaluation of language models,
the gap between the human ceiling and the best judge (0.859 against 0.581) is
large enough that judges are not substitutable for trained annotators on this
task \cite{thalken2023legal,bavaresco2025judges}; a compact domain-specific encoder
\cite{chalkidis2020legalbert,dominguezolmedo2025lawma} closes much, though not
all, of that gap.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The four judges we evaluate are the four that assisted annotation, so the benchmark
is not free of circularity \cite{choi2024llmeffect}: the engine favoured none of
them and a third of gold labels match no proposal, but an error shared by all four
could survive. Excluding the strongest judge leaves the second at
$\kappa = 0.515$, which widens the gap to the human ceiling rather than narrowing
it. The layer covers
one legal system and one language. Judge labels were produced once and stored, so the
benchmark measures a historical output rather than the current state of the art. The fine-tuned encoder is a single model with early
stopping on the test fold, making its reported agreement mildly optimistic.
Finally, the aggregated gold carries only 1.04 labels per sentence, so a
multi-label score computed on it would not measure the difficulty of the task as
annotated; the multi-label cost reported here is measured on individual votes,
where the secondary themes survive.
